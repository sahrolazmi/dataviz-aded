    Module 1

    Why we visualise data

    Data visualisation means showing numbers as pictures, such as charts, graphs, maps and tables, so people understand them quickly.

    A good chart cuts the "time to insight". The reader sees a trend, a cluster or an outlier in seconds instead of reading a long line list.

    In public health, charts are not decoration. They guide action. An epidemic curve tells us if an outbreak is growing. A map tells us where to send a team. A clear chart can help a decision maker act early, and a bad chart can lead them to the wrong decision.

    Two big reasons

    Explore

    Quick charts for yourself, to understand the data, check for errors and find what is interesting. They do not need to be pretty.

    Explain

    A polished chart that tells one clear story to a specific audience. Most charts in reports and slides are this type.

    Five jobs a chart does

    PurposePublic health example
    Identify patternsA sudden spike in daily dengue cases, or a cluster of cases in one locality
    Communicate complex informationAn infographic on measles vaccination coverage for parents
    Support decisionsA district map that shows where to target fogging or vaccination
    Monitor progressWeekly TB treatment success rate against the national target
    Keep a historical recordThe full epidemic curve of an outbreak in the final report

    Before you draw, ask four questions

    1. Who is my audience? An epidemiologist, a director, or the public?
    2. What do I want them to know or do?
    3. How will I show it? A number, a table, a chart, a map, or text?
    4. What data do I have to support the message?
    Module 2

    Know your data type first

    The type of data decides the type of chart. So the first step is always to ask: "What kind of variable am I showing?"

    Data typeWhat it isExamplesUsually shown with
    NominalGroups with no orderState, sex, type of pathogenBar chart, table
    OrdinalGroups with a natural orderAge group, severity (mild, moderate, severe)Bar chart in the natural order
    Discrete numericalCounts in whole numbersNumber of cases, admissionsBar chart, histogram, epi curve
    Continuous numericalMeasurements on a scaleAge in years, BMI, incubation periodHistogram, box plot, scatter plot
    TimeValues at regular time pointsDaily cases, epi weekLine graph, epi curve
    GeographicValues linked to a placeIncidence rate by districtChoropleth map, dot map
    Part of a wholeShares that add up to 100%Cases by serotypeBar, stacked bar, waffle chart
    RelationshipTwo measures on the same unitVaccination coverage vs incidence by districtScatter plot

    Simple tip: if you can put the values in alphabetical order without losing meaning, the data is nominal. If the order matters, like age groups, keep that order in the chart.

    Choosing the right chart

    Use a four-step strategy: find the type of data, decide the purpose, know the audience, then pick the chart.

    Displays at a glance

    DisplayBest forWatch out
    Big numberOne or two key numbersYou lose context
    TableLooking up exact valuesPoor on live slides; right-align numbers
    Heat mapHigh and low values in a big tableOne colour scale, with a legend
    Bar chartComparing groupsStart at zero; sort by value; horizontal for long names
    Grouped barTwo or three sub-groupsToo many series is hard to read
    Stacked barTotals plus their partsOnly the bottom segment is easy to compare

    Module 4

    The epidemic curve

    The epi curve is the most important chart in outbreak work. It is a histogram of cases by date or time of onset.

    From its shape, the team can guess how the disease is spreading:

    • Point source: one sharp peak. Everyone was exposed at one time, such as one meal.
    • Continuous common source: a plateau. People keep being exposed, such as a leaking water pipe.
    • Person-to-person: waves of growing peaks, about one incubation period apart.

    Module 5

    Good practice: the "Do" list

    A good chart shows one clear message, is honest about the data, and makes sense without the speaker.

    The Do list

    • Know your audience. Experts can read a box plot. The public needs a simple bar chart or infographic with plain words.
    • Choose the right chart. Bar for comparison, line for trend, map for place, histogram for distribution.
    • Make it clear. A full title with what, where and when. Label both axes with units. Add the data source and date.
    • Give context. Show rates when you compare places of different size. Add a target, baseline or national average.
    • Put the message in the title. "Cases fell by 40% after week 32" tells the reader what to see.
    • Highlight what matters. One strong colour for the key bar, grey for the rest. The eye notices colour, size and position before it reads.
    • Label directly. Put labels next to lines or bars so the eye does not jump to a legend.
    • Keep text readable. One sans-serif font, no ALL CAPS, no diagonal text, 18 points or more on slides.
    • Use colour carefully. Few colours, no neon. Use colour-blind-friendly pairs such as blue and orange instead of red and green.
    • Be ethical. No names or home locations on spot maps. Never label an outbreak by an ethnic or religious group.

    Real examples of good practice

    John Snow's cholera map, London, 1854. Snow marked each cholera death on a street map. The deaths clustered around the Broad Street water pump. The map helped convince officials to remove the pump handle.

    Sorted bar chart of rates by state. The course slides show XYZ rates by state in Malaysia, 2024, sorted from highest (Pahang, 6.23 per 100,000) to lowest (Johor, 0.10), with the national rate (0.56) shown as its own bar.

    Table to heat map (DDC Thailand). A plain table of dengue case characteristics was improved by removing heavy borders, aligning numbers to the right and adding colour shading.

    Bad practice: how charts mislead

    Module 7

    Tools and ways to present

    The best tool is the one your team can use well and repeat every week. Start simple, and move to code or dashboards when you need speed, repeatability or interactivity.

    Ways to present

    ModalityUse it forKey rule
    Static chartsReports, articles, situation reportsMust make sense without a speaker
    SlidesBriefingsOne message per slide, large text
    Infographics and postersThe public and social mediaPlain words, icons, one or two big numbers
    Interactive dashboardsDaily monitoring (for example, Malaysia's COVIDNOW)Let users pick time and place
    MapsField teams and planningRates, not counts; protect privacy
    Animated chartsChange over timeKeep it short; give a pause button

    Whatever the tool, the rules still apply. A beautiful dashboard with a cut axis is still misleading.
